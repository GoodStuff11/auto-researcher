## Leaf Status Summary

* **Question**: Can VMC optimization be made to change the sign structure of a fermionic NQS cheaply, rather than staying in the basin set by the initial mean-field state?
* **Answer**: Not known yet, but on the clusters we could test there was no wrong-sign trap. Networks trained without the ground state's symmetries kept the symmetry breaking of their mean-field start. With symmetries built in, the sign error on a 16-site cluster fell about 3-fold as the network doubled in size. On a 12-site cluster even a fit aimed straight at the exact signs got only about 20% below ordinary training, so network size, not the optimiser, sets the limit. The direct 16-site trap test (about 250 CPU-hours) was judged predictable and not run.
* **What we showed**: The apparent traps of networks trained without symmetry were leftover symmetry breaking from the mean-field start: trained with the lattice symmetry built in, they reached the exact signs on a 12-site cluster, including one whose nearest competing state was close in energy. On a 16-site doped cluster, the sign error fell from 0.019 to 0.007 (in units of the hopping) as the network grew from about 1300 to 2600 parameters, and a network first pulled toward the exact state and then trained normally ended at the same level as ordinary training. On a 12-site cluster, neither first- nor second-order fitting could hold a state given the exact signs below ordinary training by more than about a fifth.
* **What it would take**: The direct test on the 16-site cluster: start the network with better signs than ordinary training reaches and see whether it stays there (about 250 CPU-hours, predicted with 80% confidence to drift back). Beyond about 20 sites, where traps might differ, the test needs sampled rather than exact training and has not been designed.
* **Conclusion**: At exact-diagonalization size, no wrong-sign basin was found for a symmetry-projected neural-network backflow state. The residual sign error on the 4×4 open $t$–$t'$–$J$ instance falls as the network widens, and on a 12-site toy even a second-order fit aimed straight at the exact signs gets only 22% below the cold start: the lever is expressivity, not the optimiser. The one direct basin probe on the 4×4 instance (about 250 CPU-h) was refused by the compute gate as predictable, so that instance's basin question is closed at this budget, not answered.
* **Scope**: projected NNBF (width 3 to 10) with exact-force stochastic reconfiguration; the sign half on one 4×4 OBC $t$–$t'$–$J$ instance ($6+6$, $t'=-0.25$, $J=0.4$) and on a 4×3 open $t$–$J$ toy ($5+5$); the competitor half on 4×3 tori at in-sector gaps of $0.234t$ and $0.273t$ and on the toy at $0.12t$. Exact sums or exact forces throughout; nothing beyond 16 sites.
* **Compute spent**: 2,233 CPU-h in five Slurm jobs (410199, 451976, 507178, 507179, 625182), plus 0.3 CPU-h of single-thread T1 runs; all predates the compute gate.

**The question.** A neural quantum state (NQS) returns the amplitude $\psi_\theta(\mathbf n)$ of each occupation configuration $\mathbf n$; for fermions on doped 2D lattices the signs are the hard part. The ansatz is neural-network backflow (NNBF: a Slater determinant whose orbitals depend on the whole configuration through a small network), trained by stochastic reconfiguration (SR), a natural-gradient method that acts like a small imaginary-time step and never flips a sign directly. A network started from unrestricted Hartree–Fock (UHF) might stay in a *wrong-sign basin*: signs worse than the best the network could hold. If such basins exist, a cheap optimiser fix beats a bigger network. Sign quality is $\Delta E_{\mathrm{sign}}=E_{\mathrm{sign}}-E_0$ ($E_0$ exact; $E_{\mathrm{sign}}$ the lowest energy with the trained signs fixed and the best non-negative amplitudes). "Projected" means $\psi_P=P_\chi\psi_\theta$ carries the ground state's lattice-symmetry labels $\chi$.

**The chain of evidence.** (1) The early basins were symmetry contamination: on a 4×3 Hubbard torus an unprojected width-32 NNBF had $\Delta E_{\mathrm{sign}}=0.082t$; projected training from the same UHF start reached $5\times10^{-6}t$ at width 8 (407525). On the smallest-gap torus ($\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}=0.234t$) all nine projected cold runs reached $\psi_0$ (410199). (2) On the 4×4 OBC cluster (dimension 1,681,680) projected NNBF keeps $\Delta E_{\mathrm{sign}}=0.0125t$ at width 6 (451706). Three cold arms and an oracle arm W (fidelity-trained toward $\psi_0$, then released) got equal energy steps; the best cold error was 0.0192, 0.0117, 0.0070 at widths 4, 6, 10 (1284, 1734, 2634 parameters) and W ended at 0.0189, 0.0115, 0.0070 (507179, 625182). Every width stayed formally unconverged, so the pre-registered width-trend tie-break returned CAPACITY. (3) Because W never started below the cold level, two toy probes placed the cold amplitudes with the exact signs directly: on a projected 4×3 $t$–$J$ toy (675 parameters) first-order SR reached 0.0092 against cold 0.0109 and Levenberg–Marquardt (second order) 0.0085, far from the threshold 0.0055 (R7.1); released, they returned to the cold level. At $\Delta_{\mathrm{in}}=0.12t$ no cold run was captured by the competitor (R7.3).

**Why the alternatives fail.** Orbital swaps cannot rank basins (R2); fixed-node acceptance reuses a killed estimator (R2.1); deflation and orthogonal probes are SCF metadynamics (R2.2, R3.2); sampler changes cannot move SR's fixed points (R3); continuation keeps the metastable branch (R1, R4, R3.3); better local optimisers reach the same basin (R2.4, R7.2); tempering needs an absent basin (R7.5); projection-cost ideas need sampling (R3.4, R7.4); R3.1 lost its premise.
