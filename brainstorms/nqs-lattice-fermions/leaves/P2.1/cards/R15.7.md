# R15.7 — Multiple-try Metropolis on cheap candidate ratios (Round 15.7) (KILLED)
* **The idea**: The all-sites route's ratios are about 40 times cheaper than `dense2`'s, which pays an LU per candidate. Multiple-try Metropolis evaluates $M$ candidate hops per step and picks one by weight, so the route could spend its cheap ratios on better-chosen moves and lower the autocorrelation time per sweep, an advantage `dense2` cannot afford.
* **Lineage**: Operation B (active probing) and a cross-field translation from Monte Carlo statistics, Round 15. No successor.
* **Source analogy**: source problem: random-walk Metropolis mixes slowly. Correspondence: candidate hops = trial points; carried-inverse ratios = target-density evaluations. Why it worked there: several cheap trial evaluations per step increase the accepted step's quality when evaluations are cheap relative to the step. Translation gap: here the expensive part is the accepted update, not the evaluation. Citation: Liu, Liang and Wong, J. Am. Stat. Assoc. 95, 121 (2000).
* **Outcome**: KILLED at G4 (2026-11-17, T0).
* **Kill rule**: Gate 4: can the mechanism lower the route's cost per decorrelated sample at $L=16$ by the needed factor?
* **Evidence**: on the R13.1 engine about 74% of the sweep is accepted-move updates ($127\times7.9\times10^5$ of $1.35\times10^8$ multiply-adds), and on the 11c engine about 70% (R13.6). With acceptance 0.15 the route spends about 6.6 ratios per accepted move; multiple-try Metropolis with $M$ candidates needs $2M$ ratios per step (forward and reference sets), more per accepted move. A single-hop accepted move moves one electron by one bond whichever candidate is chosen, so decorrelation per accepted move, which sets the dominant cost, does not grow with $M$; selecting by $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ weight favours high-weight, often reversing, moves.
* **Why this kills it**: the gain is bounded by the ratio share of the sweep (at most about 1.35 times, the R13.6 bound), and multiple tries increase that share instead of shrinking it.
* **What the kill does not rule out**: multiple tries over composite moves that decorrelate more per accepted update than their update rank costs; delayed acceptance for families where ratios dominate (R13.6's open scope).
* **Strongest case against the kill**: trained states may have much larger integrated autocorrelation times than the untrained ones measured (about 1 to 1.8 sweeps), where better proposal choice could matter; unresolved, but any gain must still come per accepted update, which the argument bounds.
* **Checked by**: self-gated by the round-15 route generator (2026-11-17, quick literature search found no multiple-try Metropolis in NQS VMC); no per-leaf critic yet.
* **Lesson**: route-specific: the accepted update, not the ratio, dominates this family's sweep (the same mechanism as R2.5 and R13.6), which is a property of this engine, not a general lesson.
