* **Question**: Can a deliberately biased pair of runs (initialized or constrained toward each competing order, e.g. filled versus half-filled stripe) be turned into a controlled decision rule using energy-variance extrapolation of each branch to zero variance, in the spirit of level-crossing detection?
* **Answer**: No, not beyond comparing the two energies. Extrapolating each run to zero energy variance is already standard and cannot detect the competing state mixed into each run. On one 12-site Hubbard cluster where a stripe state and a near-Néel state cross exactly, every rule built from the two trained runs gave the same verdict as picking the lower energy. That choice, abstaining within error bars, never erred on well-trained runs but erred once in 9 on deliberately mistrained ones. The key case was judged predictable and not run.
* **What we showed**: On a 12-site Hubbard cluster tuned so that a stripe state and a near-Néel state of the same symmetry cross exactly, every rule that rotates within the two trained runs picks the run with the lower trained energy (a two-state algebra argument, confirmed on 9 of 9 real cases), so it can only be as good as the energy comparison. Whether the energy comparison is right was decided by network size: the larger network was right on every case, the smaller one wrong on 2 of 3 seeds. One exact Lanczos step corrected most wrong calls, but with realistic sampling noise it abstained almost everywhere for energy differences below the competing-order scale.
* **What it would take**: The case left open is a pair whose network bias points against the truth at the competing-order energy scale, trained, saved and sampled realistically on a second cluster (about 300 CPU-hours; predicted to fail with 85% confidence). A decision rule that uses information other than the two energies and stays affordable as the cluster grows would reopen the question; none is known.
* **Conclusion**: On one 12-site Hubbard torus with an exact same-symmetry level crossing, no rule built from trained branch energies or from Ritz rotations of trained states beats the variational bias of the branches: the decision is either easy (enough capacity) or undecidable from energies. A plain final-energy comparison that abstains within its sampling error never committed wrong on the trained final states (job 619080) or on the Hard-Core-scale replicates; on adverse-bias snapshot states it committed wrong on 1 of 9 (seed 3456 at $d=-0.0002$, truth $B$). One exact Lanczos step fixes most wrong calls, but sampled at $2\times10^4$ draws per branch it abstains almost everywhere below the Hard-Core gap scale. The case that matters most (a bias pointing against the truth at the Hard-Core gap scale, on trained and saved states) was not measured: the compute gate refused it as predictable, so the leaf is closed at this budget, not answered.
* **Scope**: $4\times3$ periodic Hubbard torus, $4+4$ (doping $1/3$), $U=8t$, $t_y=1.11394t$, $t'$ swept through one same-irrep conical intersection between a period-4 spin stripe and a near-Néel state (not stripe against $d$-wave); space-group-projected NNBF at widths 4 and 8 (676 and 1160 parameters against a projected sector dimension of 1618, so width 8 is in the sector-fitting regime); exact sums for training; sampled decisions with i.i.d. draws only.
* **Compute spent**: 1,564 CPU-h (410917: 67; 451852: 1,412; 619080: 84) plus about 7 CPU-minutes interactive; all pre-gate.

**The question.** In the doped Hubbard model competing orders lie within $\lvert\delta\rvert/V\sim10^{-3}t$ per site of each other (the *Hard-Core scale*), below typical variational errors. Practice trains two runs, each seeded toward one order, and compares energies; each run $X$ carries a bias $b_X\ge0$, so the comparison decides by the sign of $\delta+\Delta b$ ($\delta=E_B-E_A$, $\Delta b=b_B-b_A$). The leaf asks whether such a pair can become a rule that is sound, gains power as variance shrinks, costs a constant factor, and does not depend on the seeds.

**The test bed.** A two-parameter scan found an exact same-irrep conical intersection at $t'_c=-0.470482$ (different-irrep competitors separate trivially, R1.2). With $d=t'-t'_c$ the ground state is a spin stripe ($B$) for $d<0$ and near-Néel ($A$) for $d>0$; the in-irrep gap is $1.4\times10^{-3}t$ at $d=\pm0.0002$ and $0.025t$ at $\pm0.005$. Truth is ED; branches are projected neural-network backflow (NNBF) trained by stochastic reconfiguration.

**The chain of evidence.**
1. *Transporting easy states fails*: continuation from snapshots far from the crossing was wrong on 5 of 15 width-4 pairs (451852; R3.5).
2. *Capacity decides the easy case.* The lower final energy wins on all 6 targets for all 3 seeds at width 8, but is wrong on 2 of 3 seeds at width 4.
3. *The $2\times2$ lemma.* Every energy-based rule has its boundary at $\delta+\Delta b=0$, and the lowest eigenvector of a real symmetric $2\times2$ matrix lies mostly on the lower diagonal, so Ritz rules return the lower trained energy; off-diagonal information is bounded by $W\approx5\times10^{-4}t$, against $\Delta b=3.6$–$5.4\times10^{-3}t$.
4. *It holds on real states* (619080): Ritz equalled the lower-diagonal prediction on 9 of 9 (right on 4); the naive rule with final-state sampling error never committed wrong (2 commits, 7 abstentions); an exact block-Lanczos step was right on 9 of 9.
5. *Sampling removes the Lanczos gain below the Hard-Core scale*: on snapshot states an exact step fixes 4 of 6 wrong calls; sampled it commits on under 2% of replicates (R6.5). At $d=\pm0.005$ sampled plain energies commit right on 100%, but the bias there pointed toward the truth.

**Why the alternatives fail.** Zero-variance extrapolation echoes the seed (R1.1, R3.4); gap certificates are powerless where gaps close (R1.3, R3.3, R5.1–R5.3); cross-order overlaps cost $e^{\mathcal O(V)}$ samples (R1.4a, R7.2, R1.4, R2.1, R2.2); probes see only biased energies (R2.3, R3.1, R7.4); R7.1, R7.3, R7.5 fail on size.

**Beyond this leaf** (`library.md`): L16 (Ritz returns the lower trained energy); L17 (probes measure $E_X+b_X$, never $\delta$); L7; L10; L42; L37, L46, L45, L6, L44.

**Strongest objection.** One 12-site crossing, an easy width in the sector-fitting regime, adverse bias measured only below the Hard-Core scale: "capacity decides" may be an ED-size artefact (cross-node Majors 2, 6). *Partial answer:* the lemma is algebraic wherever two diabats dominate, and the samples needed, $n\approx8\sigma^2/\delta^2$, fall as $1/V$. The adverse-bias case is unanswered, hence "at this budget".

**Limits.** See Scope; snapshot states stand in for unsaved trained states in the falsifier.
