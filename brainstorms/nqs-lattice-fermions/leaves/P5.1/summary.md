## Leaf Status Summary
* **State**: OPEN
* **Updated**: 2026-11-18 (round 15: R51.11 ladder and R51.7 decay test submitted; per-leaf critic round 15 applied, both rules amended before any output was read; `log/round-15.md`, `log/round-15-critic.md`).
* **Question**: Can a determinant- or Pfaffian-based fermionic NQS be optimised to within a small fraction of the lowest energy its own parameters can represent, at a sampled cost per step that grows polynomially with system size and with fewer total samples than stochastic reconfiguration or MinSR need for the same accuracy?
* **Answer**: Not known. Stochastic reconfiguration (SR, the standard natural-gradient optimiser) with flattened sampling (a published sampler) came within about 2% of a noise-free optimised state on one 12-site state and needed at least 3 times fewer samples than standard SR; over the one size step measured (10 to 12 ladder sites) its samples per step grew 3.8 times, as fast as the number of configurations the state occupies, but with one seed and a fixed step length that is about 3 to 14 times longer, relative to the remaining gain, on the 10-site cluster than on the 12-site one, so size and step length are not separated, and no third size was bought. MinSR, which is SR with the step solved in sample space, was behind both flattened and standard SR at equal samples on one 10-site test with fewer samples than parameters, at the best of four step lengths (one seed), before either reached the target accuracy.
* **What we showed**: On a 12-site doped Hubbard network state, SR with flattened sampling and a fixed step fell short of a reference state polished by noise-free exact-sum optimisation (a different optimiser) by 23%, 9% and 2% of that state's gain at 16,000, 66,000 and 262,000 samples per step, so noise slows the path. At equal total samples it reached 90% of the noise-free gain with 3.3 to 9.4 times fewer samples than SR drawing from $|\psi|^2$ on three clusters of 10 and 12 sites. To come within 10% of its noise-free run it needs 31,280 samples per step on a 10-site two-leg ladder and 118,600 on the 12-site one: 3.8 times more, against 1.7 times for the cube of the size and 3.5 times for the occupied configurations (one seed at 12 sites; provisional, since a second seed was called for and not run). The step length was fixed in the metric: both sizes bounce with period two, but one step's first-order gain is 3 to 14 times larger relative to the gap at 10 sites than at 12 (mean 0.30 of the gap at 10 sites; two states, 0.02 and 0.12, at 12), so the 3.8 mixes size with step regime. Generic noise-reduction tricks for the gradient gave no net gain at equal cost: control variates built from the Hamiltonian and averaging over each move's neighbours were measured on 10 and 12 sites, and symmetry-paired samples and estimates across sizes were ruled out by argument; only reshaping the sampling distribution helped.
* **What it would take**: First, the repeat seed at 12 sites (about 3 CPU-hours) that would confirm or demote the 3.8× growth. A third size: the same fixed-step comparison on a 14-site two-leg ladder costs about 160 CPU-hours over 5–6 days, mostly for its noise-free reference, and was declined at this budget because its likeliest result would not settle the trend; a cheaper noise-free reference at 14 sites or a larger budget would reopen it. Cheaper checks at 10 and 12 sites can say whether the growth belongs to the sampler or to the step (about 4–6 CPU-hours each), and a comparison with the step length matched in regime across sizes (for example equal first-order gain per step) is needed before any size ratio is read as a size effect; a 12-site repeat of the MinSR test (about 4 CPU-hours, not proposed: its likely result changes nothing); then one run at 24 sites.
