* **Question**: Can a determinant- or Pfaffian-based fermionic NQS be optimised to within a small fraction of the lowest energy its own parameters can represent, at a sampled cost per step that grows polynomially with system size and with fewer total samples than stochastic reconfiguration or MinSR need for the same accuracy?
* **Answer**: Not known. On one 12-site state, standard sampled stochastic reconfiguration (SR) with a fixed short step got within about 2% of what exact optimisation reaches, at about 100 samples per parameter per step, but how that need grows with size is not yet measured. No optimiser has yet been compared with standard sampled SR or MinSR at equal total samples; the runs so far measure how standard SR's shortfall falls with samples.
* **What we showed**: For a 12-site doped Hubbard network state, SR with a fixed step and SR whose step size is chosen on separate samples progress at the same rate at equal samples through 60 steps; the fixed step simply keeps going. After 120 fixed steps, its shortfall to the exactly optimised state fell from 23% to 9% to 2% of the reachable gain as the samples per step went from 16,000 to 66,000 to 262,000. Halving the step instead removed only 14% of the shortfall, so sampling noise slows the path rather than leaving a fixed jitter around the minimum. At equal total samples, fewer samples per step did better within 120 steps, but only the larger batches reached 90% of the exactly optimised value.
* **What it would take**: The same fixed-step training against its own noise-free (exact-sum) version on 10-, 12- and 14-site ladders (first stage running), to see whether the samples needed per step grow like a low power of the size or like the number of configurations the state occupies; the same runs with plain, unflattened sampling, including fewer samples than parameters, where MinSR's step differs from SR's, give the comparison with SR and MinSR in total samples; then one run at 24 sites.

### The question and the argument
A fermionic neural quantum state (NQS) is trained by variational Monte Carlo: each step draws $N$ configurations, estimates the energy gradient (the force $f$) and the metric $S$ (the covariance of the log-derivatives of the wave function), and moves along the natural gradient $S^{-1}f$ (SR; MinSR is the same step solved in sample space, identical when $N\ge P$, the parameter count). On the 12-site test state, sampled SR with a flattened ("guided") sampling distribution was stopped after 60 steps at 51% of the gap to the exact energy, while exact-sum optimisation reaches 75% (L61). This optimiser (fixed-step SR, as in NetKet and the MinSR papers, with overdispersed sampling, arXiv:2507.05352) is standard practice.

*Fixed step, sampling-limited (R51.1).* Fixed-step and held-out-step SR progress at the same rate at equal samples through 60 steps; the fixed step simply keeps going. With the step fixed, the 120-step shortfall fell as $N^{-0.87}$ and barely responded to halving the step, so noise slows the path. Fewer samples per step won at equal total samples within 120 steps (1.97M draws: 0.587 at $N$, 0.516 at $4N$, 0.403 at $16N$), yet only $4N$ and $16N$ reached 90% of the exact-sum value. A held-out acceptance test never fires at this $N$ (R51.6 killed); its proposed lesson ("selection makes the floor") is withdrawn.

*The size question (R51.8, R51.10; R51.9 killed).* Polynomial cost is decided by how the needed $N$ grows with size against each size's noise-free run; one-step proxies missed the multi-step need by 2–4× both ways (R51.9). Ridge-regression theory predicts it from one state ($\kappa$). The cheap 12-site step relies on repeated draws, which vanish once the state's effective support $e^{H}$ exceeds $N$.

*Alternatives.* Median-of-means over sample blocks (R51.5) was reopened: its kill used a one-step probe, while fixed-step SR at 6.2 samples per parameter descends steadily and near-node forces are infinite-variance (Trail, PRE 77, 016703, 2008). Step decay with averaging (R51.7) is shelved until a run levels off. Leverage-sampled metric rows (R51.3) cut the cost of exact-sum optimisation, not samples.

*Strongest objections.* (1) Nothing yet compares any optimiser with SR or MinSR at equal total samples; R51.8's plain-sampling arms, including $N<P$, are the first. (2) The size test spans 10 to 14 sites, with the 10-site instance easier; it can exclude support-like growth, not prove a polynomial.

*Limits.* Exact i.i.d. draws (no Markov-chain correlation), one seed per arm, one network family, no 24-site evidence.
