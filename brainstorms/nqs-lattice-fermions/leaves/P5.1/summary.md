## Leaf Status Summary
* **State**: OPEN
* **Updated**: 2026-11-18 (round 17: R51.32 regime-matched size test POLY-CONSISTENT, job 773759; R51.8's support-like step demoted to "at one fixed step length"; `log/round-17.md`).
* **Question**: Can a determinant- or Pfaffian-based fermionic NQS be optimised to within a small fraction of the lowest energy its own parameters can represent, at a sampled cost per step that grows polynomially with system size and with fewer total samples than stochastic reconfiguration or MinSR need for the same accuracy?
* **Answer**: Not known. Stochastic reconfiguration (SR, the standard natural-gradient optimiser) with flattened sampling (a published sampler) came within about 2% of a noise-free optimised state on one 12-site state and needed at least 3 times fewer samples than standard SR; over the one size step measured (10 to 12 ladder sites) its samples per step grew 1.2 times, less than the cube of the size, when the step length was set so one step makes the same fraction of progress at both sizes, against 3.8 times at one fixed step length that is too long for the smaller cluster; one size step of a factor 1.2 cannot establish polynomial growth, and no third size was bought. MinSR, which is SR with the step solved in sample space, was behind both flattened and standard SR at equal samples on one 10-site test with fewer samples than parameters, at the best of four step lengths (one seed), before either reached the target accuracy.
* **What we showed**: On a 12-site doped Hubbard network state, SR with flattened sampling and a fixed step fell short of a reference state polished by noise-free exact-sum optimisation (a different optimiser) by 23%, 9% and 2% of that state's gain at 16,000, 66,000 and 262,000 samples per step, so noise slows the path. At equal total samples it reached 90% of the noise-free gain with 3.3 to 9.4 times fewer samples than SR drawing from $|\psi|^2$ on three clusters of 10 and 12 sites. To come within 10% of its noise-free run it needs 31,280 samples per step on a 10-site two-leg ladder and 118,600 on the 12-site one: 3.8 times more, against 1.7 times for the cube of the size and 3.5 times for the occupied configurations, but at one fixed step length whose first-order gain per step is 3 to 14 times larger relative to the gap at 10 sites than at 12. With the 10-site step halved so one step gains the same fraction of the gap as at 12 sites (0.070 against 0.069), the 10-site need is 98,400 and the ratio 1.21, below the cube of the size (two seeds at the deciding 10-site rungs, one seed at 12; the 12-site step regime rests on two states). Generic noise-reduction tricks for the gradient gave no net gain at equal cost: control variates built from the Hamiltonian and averaging over each move's neighbours were measured on 10 and 12 sites, and symmetry-paired samples and estimates across sizes were ruled out by argument; only reshaping the sampling distribution helped: drawing in proportion to the wavefunction's magnitude needed 3.8 times (10 sites) and 4.2 times (12 sites) fewer samples per step than the flattened sampler at the same step length (one seed at 12 sites).
* **What it would take**: A third size with the step regime matched: a 14-site two-leg ladder costs about 160 CPU-hours over 5–6 days at a fixed step, mostly for its noise-free reference; it was declined when the 10 → 12 step looked support-like, and the polynomial-looking matched result makes it the next spend that could move the answer toward yes, if a cheaper reference or the budget allows. A second 12-site seed and a matched 12-site step-regime window would firm up the 1.21; a 12-site repeat of the MinSR test (about 4 CPU-hours, not proposed: its likely result changes nothing); then one run at 24 sites.
