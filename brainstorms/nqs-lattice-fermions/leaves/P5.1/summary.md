* **Question**: Can a determinant- or Pfaffian-based fermionic NQS be optimised to within a small fraction of the lowest energy its own parameters can represent, at a sampled cost per step that grows polynomially with system size and with fewer total samples than stochastic reconfiguration or MinSR need for the same accuracy?
* **Answer**: Not known. On one 12-site state, natural-gradient training from samples with a fixed short step got within about 2% of what exact optimisation reaches, at about 100 samples per parameter per step; how that sample need grows with system size, and whether it beats standard training in total samples, is not yet measured.
* **What we showed**: For a 12-site doped Hubbard network state, the earlier stall of sampled natural-gradient training at half the reachable gain came from its noisy choice of step size; with a fixed short step it kept improving, and after 120 steps its shortfall to the exactly optimised state fell from 23% to 9% to 2% of the reachable gain as the samples per step went from 16,000 to 66,000 to 262,000. Halving the step instead removed only 14% of the shortfall, so the loss is sampling noise slowing the path, not a fixed jitter around the minimum. At equal total samples, fewer samples per step always did better within 120 steps. Building the parameter metric from 13% of the configurations, chosen by their influence on it, reproduced the exact polishing step, but over five steps it was only 2.4 times faster than exact polishing, short of the 3 times required.

### The question and the argument
A fermionic neural quantum state (NQS) is trained by variational Monte Carlo: each step draws $N$ configurations, estimates the energy gradient (the force $f$) and the parameter metric $S$, and moves along the natural gradient $S^{-1}f$ (stochastic reconfiguration, SR; MinSR is the same step solved in sample space, identical when $N\ge P$). On the 12-site test state, sampled SR with a flattened ("guided") sampling distribution stalled at 51% of the gap to the exact energy while exact-sum polishing reaches 75% (L61).

*The stall was step selection (R51.1, R51.6).* The held-out energy used to choose the step size was noisier than one step's gain; the shortest step never raised the energy. With that step fixed and always taken, the 120-step shortfall fell as $N^{-0.87}$ with samples per step and did not respond to halving the step (R51.7 killed), so noise slows the path. Fewer samples per step won at equal total samples within 120 steps (1.97M draws: 0.587 at $N$, 0.516 at $4N$, 0.403 at $16N$).

*The size question (R51.8, R51.10; R51.9 killed).* Whether the cost stays polynomial is decided by how $N_{\rm needed}$ grows with size, measured against each size's noise-free (exact-sum) run; a one-step proxy was rejected because one-step quantities missed the multi-step need by 2–4× in both directions (R51.9). Ridge-regression theory predicts the need from one state ($\kappa$), tested against the measured ratio. The cheap per-step cost seen at 12 sites relies on repeated draws, which disappear once the state's effective support $e^{H}$ exceeds $N$.

*The metric cost (R51.2–R51.4).* The exact metric is dominated by per-configuration derivative rows; leverage-sampled rows keep the exact step (R51.3) but gained only 2.4× per CPU-minute in a like-for-like run, and NOISE-FLOOR says sampling, not metric cost, limits the sampled optimiser.

*Strongest objection.* The size test spans 10 to 12 (then 14) sites with changing geometry and doping, and the 10-site instance is easier (start 5× closer to exact, 10× more parameters per configuration); it can exclude support-like growth, not prove a polynomial. Not answered until unit 2's 6×2 control.

*Limits.* Exact i.i.d. draws (no Markov-chain correlation), one seed per arm, one network family, no 24-site evidence.
